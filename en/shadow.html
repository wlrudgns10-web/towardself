What Is the Shadow?

"I'm not that kind of person." The more often we say it, the darker our shadow grows.

The self we push away

The Swiss psychiatrist Carl Gustav Jung (1875–1961) believed the mind has a region he called the shadow. The shadow is made of the parts of ourselves we have rejected with the thought "that's not me." Traits that were never praised, that got us embarrassed, or that we learned a "good person" shouldn't have all gather here.

Someone who grew up hearing "only bad kids get angry," for example, may barely notice their own anger. The anger hasn't disappeared. It has simply moved somewhere out of sight.

The shadow isn't only bad

It's easy to think of the shadow as selfishness, jealousy or laziness. But Jung said good things hide there too. Unspoken assertiveness, unexpressed talent, a longing to play and rest freely can all become shadow. For someone taught "don't put yourself forward," leadership itself may be in the shadow.

The shadow is less "the bad me" and more "the me that hasn't been accepted yet."

Where does the shadow show up?

The shadow never shows itself directly. Instead, it leaves traces.

  • Unusually strong feelings: you get oddly angry over something small, or dislike someone for no clear reason
  • Repeating conflicts: the people change, but the same kind of argument keeps happening
  • Overly firm denial: you insist a little too hard that "I would never be like that"
  • Slips of the tongue and dreams: something you blurt out, or a dream figure, is the exact opposite of how you usually are

The most common route of all is projection: seeing in other people what we can't accept in ourselves.

Making friends with your shadow

Jung never said to get rid of the shadow. What mattered to him was recognizing it and accepting it as part of ourselves. In everyday life, you can start like this:

  1. When someone really gets on your nerves, write down in one word what bothers you about them.
  2. Quietly ask yourself: "Is there even a little of that in me?"
  3. If you find it, don't blame yourself. Just stop at "So I have that side too."

The more we recognize our shadow, the less we blame others and the more clearly we see them as they are. That is exactly what the Whisperling Test is trying to measure.
