Viktor Frankl and the Three Paths to Meaning

"He who has a why to live can bear almost any how." Frankl often quoted these words of Nietzsche.

A psychiatrist who survived the camps

Viktor Frankl (1905–1997) was a psychiatrist and neurologist in Vienna, Austria. During the Second World War he was sent with his family to concentration camps because he was Jewish, and he lost his parents and his wife. After he came back alive, he wrote a book based on that experience, known in English as Man's Search for Meaning.

In the camps, Frankl saw that some people held on even under the same suffering. They had someone waiting for them, work left to finish, or something they wanted to protect. He called this meaning, and he developed a meaning-centered form of therapy: logotherapy.

Three paths to meaning

1. Doing or creating something (creative values)

We feel meaning when we add something to the world: work, a creation, an act that helps someone. It doesn't have to be grand. Lightening a coworker's load or carefully making a meal counts too.

2. Experiencing something or meeting someone (experiential values)

We feel meaning in a beautiful view, good music, and above all in loving someone. Frankl described love as seeing another person as they truly are, including their potential. That's close to seeing a person with the projection cleared away.

3. Choosing an attitude toward what can't be avoided (attitudinal values)

Even facing pain or circumstances we can't change, we can choose the attitude with which we bear them. Frankl saw this as the last freedom that can never be taken from a person.

Finding meaning in everyday life

Before you fall asleep, look back on the day and ask:

  • When did I add something today?
  • What meeting or experience warmed my heart today?
  • What attitude did I choose today in the face of something hard?

If even one of these comes to mind, it was a meaningful day. That's why the "daily review" in your Whisperling Test results ends with "a meaningful moment."
