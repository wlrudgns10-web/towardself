Guide · The Whisperling

Who Is the Whisperling?

They only texted "k." But what you heard was "Your message is annoying." Who whispered in between?

Between what was said and what you heard

Words seem to travel straight from someone's mouth to our heart, but in reality an interpretation slips in between. Most of the time it passes without trouble, but on some days the interpretation creates a story completely different from the original words. The Whisperling Test draws this moment of interpretation as a little devil at your ear. Its name comes from what it does: it whispers, and it bends the words as it passes them on.

Where does the idea come from?

Carl Jung: what the Whisperling uses as fuel

The Swiss psychiatrist Carl Jung called it projection when people see in others the parts of themselves they haven't accepted, their shadow. Placing our ideal image (anima/animus) onto someone is projection too. The Whisperling uses this shadow and these ideals as fuel to change what we hear.

Phil Stutz and Barry Michels: giving the saboteur a name

In their book Coming Alive, the American psychiatrist Phil Stutz and psychotherapist Barry Michels called the inner enemy that holds us back and blocks our growth "Part X." According to them, everyone has a Part X and it never fully disappears, but we can build the strength to notice it and push back. The idea also reached many people through the Netflix documentary Stutz.

The Whisperling is a character that reimagines this idea for the moment of hearing someone's words in a relationship. The original Part X is described as working against our whole lives, not just relationships, so think of the Whisperling as one close-up scene from that bigger picture.

Why make it a character?

"You tend to distort what people say" is unpleasant to hear, so it's easy to brush off. But "Your Whisperling chimes in a lot" lands differently. The problem becomes something that slipped in between you and the other person, not you yourself. In therapy, separating a problem from the person like this is called externalization. Since you don't have to blame yourself, it becomes easier to look at yourself honestly.

The Whisperling's three tricks

  • Thorn: adds thorns to words. "I'm a bit busy today" sounds like "You're a bother." Its fuel is the frustration and aggression we don't want to admit: the shadow.
  • Grader: turns words into a score about you. "Can you fix this part?" sounds like "This is the best you can do?" Its fuel is the harsh inner critic. See Handling Your Inner Critic for more.
  • Halo: covers words with a sparkling meaning. "Thanks" sounds like "You're special to me." Its fuel is the anima/animus. It feels sweet, but it often comes back as disappointment. Also read Why Do the Rose-Colored Glasses Come Off?

How to handle your Whisperling

There's no need to try to get rid of it. On days when we're tired or hurt, everyone's Whisperling gets louder. What matters is noticing the whisper once before believing it as fact.

  1. Name it: when you feel shaken, say to yourself, "Ah, my Whisperling is back."
  2. Separate: write what the other person actually said and what the Whisperling added in two columns.
  3. Choose: think about what you want to protect in this relationship, and choose the response that fits. As Viktor Frankl put it, we can't choose our circumstances, but we can choose our attitude. (Handling Your Whisperling with Logotherapy)
