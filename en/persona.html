The Persona: The Social Masks We Wear

Me at work, me with friends, me with my parents. All of them are me, yet each is a little different.

What is the persona?

Persona was the word for the mask an actor wore in ancient theater. Jung borrowed it to describe the face we show the world to fit the roles we play: the friendly employee, the dependable eldest child, the friend who's always cheerful. All of these are personas.

Masks aren't bad

"Persona" can sound like "fake," but Jung believed we truly need one. A face that fits the situation lets us work, build relationships and protect each other. Not laughing loudly at a funeral, or speaking calmly in a meeting, is thanks to a healthy persona.

When it becomes a problem

Trouble starts when we confuse the mask with our face.

  • Becoming the mask: trapped in the role of "I always have to be fine," we stop noticing when we're tired or angry.
  • Having only one mask: we play the same role everywhere, so we're badly shaken when that role doesn't work.
  • A mask that's too thin: on the other hand, we can't put on a face that fits the moment, so we get hurt, or hurt others, more easily.

Persona and shadow

The brighter the persona, the darker the shadow behind it tends to become. Someone who has long worn the mask of "always the nice one" pushes their own anger or selfishness deeper down. And what we push away often looks especially large in other people. That's projection.

Wearing masks in a healthy way

  1. Think back over today. Which masks did you wear at work, with family and with friends?
  2. Write down which mask you most wanted to take off, and what feeling you were holding back behind it.
  3. Keep at least one time and one person with whom you can take the mask off. There, it's okay not to be okay.
